\begin{abstract}
Graph neural network (GNN) surrogates such as MeshGraphNets predict one response field for one
input mesh. Manufactured structures, however, often have inputs that are not measured, such as
small geometric imperfections, and one nominal design then gives a scatter of response fields
instead of one field. For an imperfection-sensitive structure such as an axially compressed
cylindrical shell, the same nominal design either stays unbuckled or buckles, depending on the
imperfection. A deterministic surrogate trained by mean squared error returns the average of the
two states, which is not a response that any analysis produces.

To generate this scatter, we propose HI-MGN-V, a generative extension of Hierarchical
Interpolating MeshGraphNets (HI-MGN). The hierarchical V-cycle processor of HI-MGN is kept, and
a latent variable modulates the node features before every message passing block through a gated
feature-wise linear modulation. During training, the latent variable is inferred from the finite
element (FE) result by a graph encoder and regularized by a maximum mean discrepancy; at
prediction time it is drawn from a flow-matching prior conditioned on the nominal design only.
The latent variable can be one vector per structure (global), a field on the coarsest clusters
of the hierarchy (local), or both (hybrid), selected by a single setting. Sampling iterates on
a 96-dimensional vector or on 256 coarse clusters instead of on the FE mesh, so every generated
field costs one forward pass of the graph network.

The model is evaluated on a shell-buckling dataset of 150 cylinders, each analysed 64 times with
a different random dimple that the model never sees, and on eight geometries outside the
training range with 256 FE analyses each. The generated scatter of the peak displacement is
compared with the FE scatter geometry by geometry and against the value that FE results alone
reach with the same number of samples. The deterministic HI-MGN and ports of two published
generative mesh surrogates, LDGN and SAR, are compared under the same protocol.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item HI-MGN-V generates complete response fields whose scatter comes from an unmeasured input.

\item A latent variable modulates every block of a hierarchical MeshGraphNets processor.

\item Global, local and hybrid latent placements are one setting of one implementation.

\item A flow-matching prior samples on a coarse latent instead of on the fine finite element mesh.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

As the fields of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have progressed rapidly,
efforts to build ML-based physics surrogate models have also increased. Earlier methods mainly
applied classical regressors such as Gaussian process regression and radial basis function
regression to linearly reduced subspaces such as proper orthogonal decomposition
\cite{Xiao:2019a,Moosavi:2018a,lee2020time}. Following these regressors, feed-forward neural
networks \cite{Hesthaven:2018a,lee2021parametric}, long short-term memory networks
\cite{mohan2018deep}, and variational autoencoders (VAEs) \cite{lee2024fsi,lee2024hvae} were
considered upon the linear subspace. As ML methods improved, fully data-driven nonlinear models
such as autoencoders \cite{kadeethum2022reduced,lee2020model} and VAEs
\cite{solera2023beta,lee2024thesis,lee2025physics} were used as physical surrogate models. While
these methods have been successful on structured grids or fixed discretizations, they can quickly
lose effectiveness on unstructured meshes and on geometry-varying problems.

Mesh-agnostic methods have changed this landscape by allowing different meshes for training and
inference. Point-set networks such as PointNet and PointNet++ were among the first to be used as
mesh-agnostic surrogate models \cite{qi2017pointnet,qi2017pointnet++}. Operator-learning methods
approximate the solution operator that maps input functions to output functions: DeepONet and
its geometry-aware extensions \cite{lu2021learning,he2024geom}, Geo-FNO, which maps an irregular
domain to a latent regular one \cite{li2023fourier}, and geometry-informed neural operators
\cite{li2023geometry}. Transformer-based solvers such as Transolver communicate through learned
physical slices instead of explicit connectivity \cite{wu2024transolver}, at a computational cost
that can be large compared with existing methods \cite{alkin2025ab}. As meshes in numerical
simulations are naturally graphs, GNNs that treat the simulation mesh as a graph have also been
investigated, starting from MeshGraphNets (MGN) \cite{pfaff2021mesh}. Since message passing
propagates information only one hop per block, multiscale graph methods such as MultiScale MGN
and the Bi-Stride Multi-Scale GNN (BSMS-GNN) \cite{fortunato2022multiscale,cao2023efficient},
partitioning approaches such as X-MeshGraphNet \cite{nabian2024x}, and global processors and
graph rewiring such as MeshGraphNet-Transformer and PIORF
\cite{iparraguirre2026meshgraphnet,yu2025piorf} have been proposed to improve long-range
interaction. In our previous work, Hierarchical Interpolating MeshGraphNets (HI-MGN) built the
hierarchy from the input mesh alone by farthest-point sampling (FPS) and Voronoi clustering, and
restored the fine resolution with a learned geometry-aware interpolation \cite{lee2026himgn}.

All of these surrogates learn a one-to-one map: one input mesh with its boundary conditions gives
one response field. This assumption holds when every input of the analysis is known. In practice,
a manufactured structure has inputs that are not measured, such as small deviations of its shape
from the drawing, and the same nominal design then gives a scatter of responses, $\by=S(c,\xi)$,
with $c$ the nominal input and $\xi$ the unmeasured one. Thin cylindrical shells under axial
compression are the classical case: their buckling load is highly sensitive to small geometric
imperfections \cite{hutchinson1970postbuckling}, and near the critical load one nominal shell
stays unbuckled under one imperfection and buckles under another. A deterministic surrogate
trained by the mean squared error (MSE) converges to the conditional mean of $\by$ given $c$.
When the responses form two clusters, the mean lies between them and is not a response that any
analysis produces. The surrogate must therefore generate the scatter itself, field by field,
from the nominal input only.

Surrogates of input scatter have been studied mainly for scalar or low-dimensional outputs.
Stochastic emulators model the response distribution of a scalar quantity
\cite{zhu2021glam,zhu2023spce,luthen2023spectral}, and engression learns a generative regression
with a scoring rule \cite{shen2025engression}. For fields, conditional VAEs and conditional GANs
were applied to random initial conditions of the Burgers equation \cite{yang2019conditional} and
to the load scatter of plates and hulls \cite{silionis2024deep}, and mixture density networks to
imperfections and initial conditions of mechanical systems \cite{han2026multimodal}. Gen-Fab and
GUST generate as-fabricated photonic devices and printed unit cells on pixel grids
\cite{azimi2026genfab,zheng2026gust}. Equivariant flow matching \cite{hendriks2025equivariant}
and Bi-FORK \cite{zimmel2026bifork} generate symmetry-breaking and bifurcating solutions. These
works either predict scalar or homogenized quantities, work on structured grids, or reproduce a
bifurcation of one nominal problem instead of the scatter of an unmeasured input over many
designs.

Generative surrogates on unstructured meshes have mostly been developed for turbulent flows.
Latent diffusion graph networks (LDGN) compress a flow field by a multiscale graph VAE and
generate it by a diffusion model on the coarse latent graph \cite{lino2025distributions}, and the
scale-autoregressive model SAR generates the latent field scale by scale with flow matching
\cite{lino2026sar}. PIFM-GN, GenCFD, turbulence and airfoil diffusion models, and probabilistic
weather models generate flow states in a similar manner
\cite{song2026pifm,molinaro2024gencfd,lienen2024turbdiff,liu2024uncertainty,oskarsson2024graphefm,price2025gencast,alet2025fgn},
and a diffusion last layer adds generated scatter to a neural operator \cite{park2026dll}. Three
properties of these models matter for the present problem. First, in most of them the scatter is
the evolution of a turbulent state in time, and the reference ensemble consists of states of
one long simulation, not of repeated analyses with random inputs. Second, the latent variable of
LDGN and SAR \emph{is} the compressed field: the generative model has to produce the whole state
on a latent graph, and the decoder only lifts it to the fine mesh. Third, sampling iterates a
network on that latent graph, whose size grows with the FE mesh, at every diffusion or
integration step. For structural meshes with tens of thousands of nodes, where most of the
response is fixed by the nominal design and only a small part is decided by $\xi$, it is more
natural to let a deterministic network compute the response from the design and to generate only
the part that $\xi$ changes.

Where the latent variable enters the network and how it is regularized are the two remaining
design choices. A \emph{global} latent is one vector per sample, as in conditional VAEs, diffusion
autoencoders \cite{preechakul2022diffae} and latent graph simulators
\cite{dahlinger2023ltsgns,nath2026uresvgae}; a \emph{local} latent is attached to positions or
regions, as in LDGN, spatially varying modulation \cite{park2019spade,zhu2020sean,tan2021inade}
and hierarchical segmentation models \cite{kohl2019hpunet,baumgartner2019phiseg}; and
\emph{hybrid} latents combine both \cite{yang2019pointflow,zeng2022lion,xie2026dualdiff}. Neither
option is ruled out by theory: a global vector can encode a location through an input-dependent
expansion \cite{park2026dll}, and local multiscale computation can reproduce long-range
correlation \cite{masuki2026rgfm}. For regularization, the $\beta$-VAE weighs a per-sample
Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence \cite{higgins2017beta}, whereas InfoVAE and the Wasserstein
autoencoder match the distribution of all latent vectors to the prior through a maximum mean
discrepancy (MMD) \cite{zhao2019infovae,tolstikhin2018wae,gretton2012kernel}; regularized and
two-stage autoencoders fit a distribution to the learned latent vectors after training
\cite{ghosh2020rae,dai2019diagnosing}.

In this work, we propose \textit{HI-MGN-V}, a generative extension of HI-MGN that produces a
scatter of complete response fields on unstructured FE meshes from the nominal design only.
HI-MGN-V preserves the encode--process--decode structure and the hierarchical V-cycle processor
of HI-MGN, and adds a latent variable that scales, shifts and gates the node features before
every message passing block. During training, a graph encoder infers the latent variable from the
FE result, and an MMD keeps the set of inferred latent vectors close to a standard normal
distribution. At prediction time, the FE result is not available, and the latent variable is
drawn from a flow-matching prior that reads only the nominal design. The latent variable can be
one vector per structure (global), a field over the coarsest clusters of the hierarchy (local),
or both (hybrid). Each choice states an assumption about how the unmeasured input acts on the
response, and all three are one setting of one implementation. Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} shows training and inference.

The main contribution of this work is a generative mesh-based surrogate in which the response
is computed by a deterministic hierarchical graph network and only the part decided by the
unmeasured input is generated. Unlike latent generative models that generate the whole field
in a latent space tied to the mesh, HI-MGN-V generates a low-dimensional latent variable that
modulates every block of the V-cycle, so the prior iterates on a 96-dimensional vector or on 256
coarse clusters, and every generated field costs one forward pass of the graph network. The
global, local and hybrid placements of the latent variable share every other component, which
allows the placement to be studied as a controlled ablation. The prior is first trained together
with the graph network and then refitted alone on standardized latent vectors of the frozen
encoder, which separates fitting the prior from learning the latent space. Throughout the
manuscript, we evaluate HI-MGN-V against the deterministic HI-MGN and against our ports of the
two closest published generative mesh surrogates, LDGN and SAR, on a shell-buckling dataset in
which the unmeasured imperfection decides whether the shell buckles. The generated scatter is
compared with the FE scatter geometry by geometry, on unseen geometries inside and outside the
training range, and against the value that FE results alone reach with the same number of
samples.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

% ======================================================================
\subsection{Model definition}\label{sec:problem}

Throughout the model, we follow the basic structure of MeshGraphNets \cite{pfaff2021mesh} and
HI-MGN \cite{lee2026himgn}. A sample is the FE mesh, treated as a graph $G=(V,E)$ with $N=|V|$
nodes and the element edges as bidirectional mesh edges. Each node stores its physical state,
the boundary and load conditions, and categorical tags such as boundary-condition types. Each mesh
edge stores the relative geometric vector between the connected nodes and its Euclidean norm,
$\{dx,dy,dz,\lVert(dx,dy,dz)\rVert\}$, evaluated in both the reference and the current
configuration, which yields eight-dimensional edge features. World edges for contact are
supported by the implementation as in HI-MGN, but the problem considered here has no contact and
uses mesh edges only.

We consider a static problem. Given the reference graph $G^{0}$, the network predicts the nodal
displacement $\mathbf{u}_i\in\R^{3}$ at the end of loading in one step, without a rollout. The nominal input $c$ consists of the reference coordinates, the shell thickness $t$,
the applied end shortening $\Delta$ (constant over nodes, stored per node) and a node type
(interior, clamped rim, loaded rim). The unmeasured input $\xi$ enters the solver's initial
geometry but not $c$, and the solver returns $\by=S(c,\xi)\in\R^{N\times3}$. The training data are
$M$ geometries with $K$ FE results each, $\{(c_m,\by_{m,k})\}_{k=1}^{K}$, and the realizations
$\xi_{m,k}$ are not given to the model. The goal is a generator
\begin{equation}
  \hat\by = D(c,\bz),\qquad \bz\sim p_\theta(\bz\mid c),
  \label{eq:generator}
\end{equation}
whose output scatter matches $p(\by\mid c)$ on geometries not used in training, including
geometries outside the training range. The latent variable $\bz$ is not an estimate of $\xi$: it
only has to describe the part of the effect of $\xi$ that is visible in the response field.

% ======================================================================
\subsection{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}

HI-MGN-V consists of three networks, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}. A hierarchical graph
network $D$ maps the nominal input and a latent variable to a displacement field. During training,
a posterior encoder $q_\phi(\bz\mid c,\by)$ infers the latent variable from the FE result. At
prediction time, the latent variable is drawn from a conditional prior $p_\theta(\bz\mid c)$ that
reads only the nominal input. The graph network never sees $\by$, and without the latent variable
it is the deterministic HI-MGN.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{figures/network.pdf}
\caption{Architecture of HI-MGN-V on the training geometry $m=137$. (a) Graph network $D$: the
28-block V-cycle over three levels of one mesh (node counts; badges: blocks), skips $s_i$, and
$\zeta_\ell$, the part of $\bz$ read at level $\ell$; the output drawn is an FE result. (b)
FPS--Voronoi hierarchy on the unrolled shell (colours: next-level clusters), and the

Part of $\bz$ read by each node. (e) Posterior encoder (training) and flow-matching prior
(inference).}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

The graph network uses the encoder--processor--decoder structure of MeshGraphNets
\cite{battaglia2018relational,sanchez2020learning,pfaff2021mesh}. Throughout the model,
multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) with SiLU activations and LayerNorm, with a latent width of
$H=128$, are used. A nodal encoder $\mathbb{E}_{V}$ maps each nodal feature to a nodal latent
vector $h_i$,
\begin{equation}
  h_i = \mathbb{E}_{V}(v_i),\qquad
  v_i = \bigl[\,\mathbf{u}^{0}_i \mid t,\Delta \mid p_i \mid \mathrm{onehot}(\text{type}_i)\,\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the state block $\mathbf{u}^{0}_i$ is zero in the static case, $(t,\Delta)$ are
input-only condition channels that the model reads but never predicts, and $p_i\in\R^{4}$ are
positional features: the distance to the centroid of the mesh, the mean length of the incident
edges, and the return probabilities of random walks of two and four steps on the mesh graph.
All continuous inputs and outputs are standardized to zero mean and unit variance with
statistics of the training split. A mesh-edge encoder $\mathbb{E}_{M}$ embeds the edge features,
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_{ij} = \mathbb{E}_{M}\bigl(e_{ij}\bigr),\qquad
  e_{ij} = \bigl[\,\mathbf{d}^{\text{cur}}_{ij},\|\mathbf{d}^{\text{cur}}_{ij}\|,
  \mathbf{d}^{\text{ref}}_{ij},\|\mathbf{d}^{\text{ref}}_{ij}\|\,\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{d}_{ij}$ is the relative position of nodes $i$ and $j$; in the static case the two
configurations coincide. A message passing (MP) block first updates the edge latent states from
their sender and receiver nodes, and then updates the node latent states from the aggregated
incoming messages,
\begin{align}
  \varepsilon_{ij} &\leftarrow \varepsilon_{ij}
    + \phi_{M}\!\big([\,h_i,\,h_j,\,\varepsilon_{ij}\,]\big),
    \label{eq:edge_update}\\[2pt]
  h_i &\leftarrow h_i
    + \phi_{V}\!\Big(\big[\,h_i,\;\textstyle\sum_j \varepsilon_{ij}\,\big]\Big),
    \label{eq:node_update}
\end{align}
where $\phi_M$ and $\phi_V$ are independently parameterized MLPs. The decoder $\mathbb{D}$, an MLP
without LayerNorm, maps the final fine-level latent state to the normalized displacement, and
the prediction is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{u}}_i = \mathcal{N}^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{D}(h_i)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}$ denotes the normalizing operator. The processor between the encoders and the
decoder is the hierarchical multi-scale processor of HI-MGN, described next, with the latent
modulation of Section~\ref{sec:modulation} inserted before every block.

% ======================================================================
\subsection{Hierarchical multi-scale processor}\label{sec:vcycle}

The processor arranges message passing over $H$ coarse graphs in a V-cycle (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}a,b). It consists of
$2H{+}1$ message passing stages, with $\{L_0,\dots,L_{2H}\}$ denoting the number of blocks at each
stage. The processor first performs $L_0$ message passes at the original level, after which the
latent states are pooled to the coarser graph. At the next level, the latent vectors are refined
for $L_1$ passes and pooled again. After the latent vectors are refined at the coarsest level,
they are upsampled by the learned, geometry-aware interpolation network, merged with the
corresponding skip states and refined once more on the way up. In this work, $H=2$ with 4096 and
256 coarse nodes, and $\{L_s\}=\{4,6,8,6,4\}$, i.e., 28 blocks in total.

The coarse graphs are built by the FPS-Voronoi strategy of HI-MGN, which requires no externally
supplied meshes (Algorithm~\ref{alg:fpsvoronoi}). First, $k$ seeds are chosen by greedy FPS on
the reference coordinates, starting from a random node. A multi-source breadth-first search over
the original mesh adjacency then assigns every node to its nearest seed in hops, giving
graph-based Voronoi clusters. The coarse nodes are the seeds; their latent features are the mean
of the latent states of their cluster, and their coordinates are inherited from the seeds. Two
coarse nodes are connected when at least one fine-mesh edge crosses between their clusters, so
the coarse connectivity follows the mesh topology and introduces no artificial connection between
nearby but unconnected parts. For $H>1$ the procedure is applied recursively. The coarse edge
features are recomputed from the coarse node positions in the same eight-dimensional form,
normalized with statistics of each level, and embedded by a separate edge encoder per level.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{FPS--Voronoi coarsening strategy of HI-MGN \cite{lee2026himgn}.}
\label{alg:fpsvoronoi}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Fine mesh graph $(V,E)$, node positions $\{p_i\}_{i\in V}$, number of coarse nodes $k$
\Statex \textbf{FPS sampling}
\State $s_1 = $ random node, \quad $S = \{s_1\}$ \Comment{$S$: set of FPS seeds}
\State $d_i = \lVert p_i - p_{s_1} \rVert,~\forall i \in V$
\For{$j = 2,\dots,k$}
    \State $s_j = \arg\max_{i \in V \setminus S} d_i$
    \State $S = S \cup \{s_j\}$
    \State $d_i = \min\!\left( d_i,\, \lVert p_i - p_{s_j} \rVert \right),~\forall i \in V \setminus S$
\EndFor
\Statex \textbf{Voronoi partitioning}
\State $C(i) = \arg\min_{a \in \{1,\dots,k\}} \mathrm{hop}(i,s_a),~\forall i \in V$ \Comment{$C$: Voronoi assignment map}
\Statex \Comment{$\mathrm{hop}(i,j)$: shortest path between nodes $i$ and $j$ in mesh edges}
\State $\mathcal{C}_a = \{\, i \in V : C(i) = a \,\},~\forall a \in \{ 1,\dots,k\}$
\Statex \textbf{Coarse graph construction}
\State $V_c = S$ \Comment{$V_c$: Coarse nodes}
\State $E_c = \left\{ (C(i),C(j)) \;\middle|\; (i,j)\in E,\ C(i)\neq C(j) \right\}$ \Comment{$E_c$: Coarse edges}
\State \Return $C,\ S\ (V_c,E_c)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Upsampling lifts the coarse latents back to the finer level with a learned, geometry-aware GNN. A
coarse-to-fine graph connects each fine node $i$ to its own coarse seed and the neighbouring
coarse nodes of that seed, forming a set of coarse source nodes $\mathcal{C}(i)$. Each source
$c\in\mathcal{C}(i)$ sends a message built from the coarse latent $h_c$, the stored skip state
$s_i$ of the fine node, and the reference-configuration offset of $i$ from the seed,
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{ic} = \psi([h_c,s_i,p_i-p_{c}]),
  \label{eq:interp_msg}
\end{equation}
and the fine latent state is reconstructed from the aggregated messages together with the skip
state, then merged with the skip state by a linear map,
\begin{equation}
  h^{\mathrm{up}}_i = \mathrm{MLP}\Bigl(\bigl[s_i,\textstyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}(i)}\mu_{ic}\bigr]\Bigr),
  \qquad
  h_i = W_{\mathrm{skip}}\,[\,s_i,\,h^{\mathrm{up}}_i\,] + b_{\mathrm{skip}}.
  \label{eq:interp_node}
\end{equation}
The forward pass of the processor with the latent modulation is summarized in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:vcycle}. The modulation $\mathrm{Mod}$ and the part $\zeta_\ell$ of the latent
variable read at level $\ell$ are defined in Sections~\ref{sec:placement} and
\ref{sec:modulation}.

Because the first FPS seed is a random node, the hierarchy depends on a random seed. During
training, two partitions with different seeds are precomputed for every sample, and one of them
is drawn at every visit, so that the model does not fit one particular partition. Validation uses
the first partition, and inference builds the hierarchy of every geometry with a fixed seed. The
clusters are therefore random patches of similar size, not a decomposition into physical regions.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Hierarchical message-passing processor of HI-MGN-V with latent modulation.}
\label{alg:vcycle}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Fine latent features $h$; latent variable $\bz$; graph edges $\{E^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=0}^{H}$;
         coarsening maps $\{C^{(\ell)}, S^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=0}^{H-1}$;
         message-passing depths $\{L_s\}_{s=0}^{2H}$
\Statex \textbf{Downsampling}
\For{$\ell = 0,\dots,H-1$}
    \For{$j = 1,\dots,L_\ell$}
        \State $h = \mathrm{Mod}_j\bigl(\mathrm{MP}_j,\ h,\ \zeta_\ell,\ E^{(\ell)}\bigr)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:mod}}
    \EndFor
    \State $h_{\mathrm{skip}}^{(\ell)} = h$
    \State $h = \mathrm{MeanPool}(h, C^{(\ell)})$
\EndFor
\Statex \textbf{Coarsest-level propagation}
\For{$j = 1,\dots,L_H$}
    \State $h = \mathrm{Mod}_j\bigl(\mathrm{MP}_j,\ h,\ \zeta_H,\ E^{(H)}\bigr)$
\EndFor
\Statex \textbf{Upsampling}
\For{$\ell = H-1,\dots,0$}
    \State $h = \mathrm{Interpolate}\bigl(h,\, h_{\mathrm{skip}}^{(\ell)},\, C^{(\ell)},\, S^{(\ell)}\bigr)$ \Comment{Eqs.~\eqref{eq:interp_msg}--\eqref{eq:interp_node}}
    \For{$j = 1,\dots,L_{2H-\ell}$}
        \State $h = \mathrm{Mod}_j\bigl(\mathrm{MP}_j,\ h,\ \zeta_\ell,\ E^{(\ell)}\bigr)$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $h$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Latent placement}\label{sec:placement}

The latent variable has up to two parts,
\begin{equation}
  \zg\in\R^{S\times D},\ S=H+1=3,\ D=32;\qquad
  \zl\in\R^{n_c\times C},\ n_c=256,\ C=4 .
\end{equation}
The global part holds one vector of length $D$ per level, and the local part one vector of length
$C$ per coarsest cluster. Each fine node $v$ has an ancestor $a_\ell(v)$ at level $\ell$ and a
coarsest ancestor $a_H(v)$. The placement selects which parts exist and defines the part
$\zeta_\ell(v)$ that node $v$ reads at level $\ell$ (Table~\ref{tab:placement}, Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}d). Blocks at a level
read that level's part, on the way down and on the way up. Where the latent variable acts states
what the model assumes about the effect of $\xi$ on the field.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Global.} The effect of $\xi$ is summarized by a few variables shared by the whole
  structure, for example whether the shell buckles and how large the deformation is. The latent
  variable is one vector per level, read by every node.
\item \emph{Local.} The effect of $\xi$ is regional: an imperfection at one place changes the
  response nearby. The latent variable is a field on the coarsest clusters, so a location can be
  written directly into it.
\item \emph{Hybrid.} Both: a shared variable and a regional field, sampled jointly.
\end{itemize}
In the shell problem $\xi$ has only three random variables, but its effect is both global (buckled
or not) and spatially structured (where the buckle lobes form and how far around the
circumference they extend). A low-dimensional $\xi$ does not imply that a global vector suffices,
and a localized effect does not imply that a local field suffices \cite{park2026dll,masuki2026rgfm}.
We therefore treat the placement as a modelling choice and study it as an ablation.

\midrule

local  & $\zl$ & $\zl[a_H(v)]$ & 4 & 1024 \\
hybrid & $\zg,\zl$ & $[\,\zl[a_H(v)]\mid\zg[\min(\ell,S-1)]\,]$ & 36 & 1120 \\

\subsection{Latent modulation of the message passing blocks}\label{sec:modulation}

Before each block $B_j$ of Algorithm~\ref{alg:vcycle} (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}c), the latent variable scales and shifts the
node features and sets how much of the block update is kept. With $h$ the node features entering
the block and $\zeta$ the part of the latent variable read by the node,
\begin{align}
  (\gamma,\ \beta,\ g) &= W_j\,\mathrm{SiLU}(\zeta) + b_j,\qquad
  \gamma,\beta,g\in\R^{H}, \label{eq:mod}\\
  \tilde h &= h\odot(1+\gamma) + \beta, \\
  h' &= h + g\odot\bigl(B_j(\tilde h)-\tilde h\bigr),
\end{align}
where $B_j(\tilde h)-\tilde h$ is the residual node update of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:edge_update} and
\eqref{eq:node_update}. The edge latent states are updated by the block as usual and are not
modulated. $W_j$ is initialized to zero and $b_j$ to $(\gamma,\beta,g)=(0,0,1)$, so at
initialization $h'=B_j(h)$: the network starts as the deterministic HI-MGN, and the latent
variable has an effect only once training changes $W_j$. This feature-wise scale and shift is
known as feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018film}. A similar form is used in
diffusion transformers \cite{peebles2023dit}, there with a normalization step and a gate that
starts at zero; here there is no normalization and the gate starts at one. The modulation costs
$28\times(\dim\zeta+1)\times3H$ parameters: 354,816 (global), 53,760 (local) and 397,824
(hybrid).

% ======================================================================
\subsection{Posterior encoder}\label{sec:encoder}

During training, the posterior encoder infers the latent variable from the FE result (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}e). The
normalized field $\by$ and the node inputs are embedded by two MLPs and fused, and three message
passing blocks on the fine mesh, without latent modulation, process the result (the encoder
trunk). Two output branches read the trunk.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Global branch.} An attention-weighted average over the nodes of each geometry,
  followed by linear maps to the mean and log-variance $\mu_g,\log\sigma_g^2\in\R^{S\times D}$.
\item \emph{Local branch.} Trunk features are mean-pooled into the coarsest clusters, two message
  passing blocks run on the coarsest graph, and linear maps give
  $\mu_\ell,\log\sigma_\ell^2\in\R^{n_c\times C}$.
\end{itemize}
The global placement uses the first branch, the local placement the second and the hybrid both.
The standard deviation is bounded below by 0.05. The latent variable is drawn as
$\bz=\mu+\sigma\odot\epsilon$ with $\epsilon\sim\N(0,I)$, which lets the training gradient pass
through the random draw \cite{kingma2014vae}.

% ======================================================================
\subsection{Regularization of the latent variable}\label{sec:reg}

A regularization term keeps the inferred latent vectors close to a standard normal distribution
$\N(0,I)$, so that they can later be generated without the FE result. The default is an MMD over
the batch; a per-sample KL divergence is implemented as the alternative.

\paragraph{MMD over the batch} The MMD compares the set of inferred vectors $\{z_i\}_{i=1}^n$ in a
batch with draws $\{e_j\}_{j=1}^n\sim\N(0,I)$ and is zero when the two sets have the same
distribution. We use the estimate that includes the $i=i'$ terms,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^2 = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,i'}k(z_i,z_{i'})
  + \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{j,j'}k(e_j,e_{j'}) - \frac{2}{n^2}\sum_{i,j}k(z_i,e_j),
\end{equation}
with a mixture of Gaussian kernels $k(a,b)=\frac15\sum_{m}\exp\bigl(-\|a-b\|^2/(c_m h)\bigr)$,
$c_m\in\{0.25,0.5,1,2,4\}$, and $h$ the median squared cross distance, held without gradient
\cite{gretton2012kernel,zhao2019infovae,tolstikhin2018wae}. For $\zg$ the estimate is computed
for each level vector over the batch and averaged over the levels. For $\zl$ every coarsest
cluster of every graph is one row, with at most 4096 rows subsampled. The rows of one geometry are
correlated, so this term matches the distribution of the four values of a cluster, pooled over
clusters; it says nothing about the correlation between clusters, which the prior of
Section~\ref{sec:prior} has to reproduce. In the hybrid the two estimates are summed. MMD acts on
the set of latent vectors and does not limit how much each vector says about its FE result, which
suits a graph network that should use the latent variable.

\paragraph{KL per sample} The KL divergence of each sample's distribution $\N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ from
$\N(0,I)$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{KL}} = \operatorname{mean}_{\text{coords}}
  \Bigl[-\tfrac12\bigl(1+\log\sigma^2-\mu^2-\sigma^2\bigr)\Bigr],
\end{equation}
is averaged over the components within each part and summed over the parts that exist.

% ======================================================================
\subsection{Conditional flow-matching prior}\label{sec:prior}

At prediction time the latent variable is drawn from a distribution conditioned on the nominal
input. It is learned by flow matching \cite{lipman2023flow,liu2023rectified}: a network $v_\theta$
learns a velocity field that moves Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ to the latent vectors seen in
training along straight paths in a pseudo-time $t\in[0,1]$. The target is the standardized latent
vector $\tilde\bz=(\bz-m)/s$ (Section~\ref{sec:training}). With one $t\sim U[0,1]$ per graph and
$\sigma_{\min}=10^{-4}$,
\begin{equation}
  \bz_t = \bigl(1-(1-\sigma_{\min})t\bigr)\epsilon + t\,\tilde\bz,\qquad
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}} = \E\,\bigl\|v_\theta(\bz_t,t,c) -
  \bigl(\tilde\bz-(1-\sigma_{\min})\epsilon\bigr)\bigr\|^2 .
\end{equation}
A sample is obtained by integrating $\dot\bz=v_\theta$ from $t=0$ to $1$ with 30 steps of Heun's
second-order method \cite{karras2022edm} and undoing the standardization. The pseudo-time enters
as sines and cosines of 16 frequencies $2^k\pi$, $k=0,\dots,15$ (32 values), denoted $\tau(t)$.
The prior reads the nominal input through its own condition trunk: node and edge encoders and
three message passing blocks of width 128 on the fine mesh, run once per geometry without $\by$.

\paragraph{Global placement} The condition trunk is reduced by an attention-weighted average to
one vector $\kappa\in\R^{128}$ per geometry. The velocity of the 96 components of $\zg$ is an MLP
of $[\operatorname{vec}(\bz_t)\mid\tau(t)\mid\kappa]$.

\paragraph{Local placement} The condition-trunk features are mean-pooled into the coarsest
clusters, $\kappa_v$ for $v\in V_H$, and the coarse edges are encoded. The node stream starts as
$x_v=\mathrm{Lin}([\zl{}_{,t}(v)\mid\kappa_v])$ and passes through four message passing blocks on
the coarsest graph, each modulated by the time embedding with the gated form of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mod}, initialized at the identity. A linear head, initialized at $0.01$ of its
default scale, gives $v_\ell(v)\in\R^{C}$.

\paragraph{Hybrid placement} The hybrid adds one vector $\omega$ per geometry that carries the
global part through the same blocks:
\begin{align}
  \omega &\leftarrow \mathrm{Lin}\bigl([\operatorname{vec}(\zg{}_{,t})\mid\kappa]\bigr), \\
  \text{before block } j:\quad
  \omega &\leftarrow \omega + \mathrm{MLP}_j\bigl([\omega\mid\bar x\mid\tau(t)]\bigr),\qquad
  x_v \leftarrow x_v + \mathrm{Lin}_j(\omega), \\
  v_g &= \mathrm{Lin}([\omega\mid\bar x]),
\end{align}
where $\bar x$ is the mean of the node stream over the clusters. The maps $\mathrm{Lin}_j$ start
at zero, so at initialization the regional part behaves as in the local placement. One
pseudo-time per geometry drives both parts, and the loss is the sum of the regional and global
flow-matching terms. The two parts are therefore generated together and exchange information at
every velocity evaluation.

% ======================================================================
\subsection{Loss and training}\label{sec:training}

All input and output channels are standardized with statistics of the training split. With the
predicted field $\hat\by=D(c,\bz)$ and $\bz$ inferred from the FE result, the loss of the joint
phase is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \alpha\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sum_{q} w_q\bigl(\hat y_{i,q}-y_{i,q}\bigr)^2
  + \lambda\,\mathcal{R}
  + \beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PV}} + w\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}},
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=1000$, $\beta=10$ and $w=1$,
normalized feature weights $w_q$ summing to one over the output channels $q$, and
$(\mathcal{R},\lambda)=(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^2,1)$ or $(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{KL}},10^{-3})$.
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PV}}$ is the squared error of the per-graph peak-to-valley
$\max_i\hat y_{i,z}-\min_i\hat y_{i,z}$ of the normalized axial displacement, an additional term
that weights an extreme value of the field. The flow-matching term is trained on a separate draw
from the encoder with $\mu$ and $\sigma$ held fixed, so it does not change the encoder. The loss
is a weighted sum chosen for the field, not the likelihood bound of a standard VAE.

Training has two phases (Algorithm~\ref{alg:train}). In the joint phase all networks train
together. At epoch $E_{\text{freeze}}$ the graph network and the encoder are frozen, and the
standardization of the flow-matching target is computed once from the latent vectors that the
frozen encoder infers over the training set,
\begin{equation}
  m=\E[\mu],\qquad s=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\mu)+\E[\sigma^2]},
\end{equation}
per component for $\zg$ and per channel for $\zl$. The prior then continues alone on
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}$ with a new optimizer and learning-rate schedule, in full precision
and without a forward pass of the graph network; the standardization values are also copied into
the exponential moving average (EMA) of its weights that is used for prediction. Fitting the
prior to the latent vectors of a frozen encoder removes the moving target that a jointly trained
prior has to follow, while the joint phase still gives the prior a good starting point. The model
with the best validation score of the joint phase is kept, and the selection restarts for the
prior-only phase. The validation score is the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) of the
normalized field, estimated per node and component with the fair ensemble estimator from 8 fields
generated with the prior.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Training HI-MGN-V (any placement).}\label{alg:train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{epoch $=1,\dots,E_{\text{freeze}}$} \Comment{joint phase}
  \For{batch $(c,\by)$, partition drawn from the hierarchy variants}
    \State $(\mu,\sigma)\gets q_\phi(c,\by)$;\ $\bz\gets\mu+\sigma\odot\epsilon$
    \State $\hat\by\gets D(c,\bz)$ \Comment{blocks modulated by $\zeta_\ell(v)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:mod}}
    \State $\mathcal{L}\gets\alpha\,\mathrm{MSE}+\lambda\mathcal{R}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PV}}
       +w\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\mathrm{sg}[\bz'])$ \Comment{$\bz'$: second encoder draw, held fixed}
    \State update all parameters
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State freeze $D$, $q_\phi$;\ compute $(m,s)$ over the training set;\ new optimizer
\For{epoch $=E_{\text{freeze}}+1,\dots,E$} \Comment{prior-only phase}
  \State update $p_\theta$ on $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}$ of standardized encoder draws
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

At prediction, for a new geometry, the hierarchy is built with the fixed seed, the condition
trunk runs once, Gaussian noise is integrated through the velocity field with 30 Heun steps, the
standardization is undone, and the graph network runs once per generated field. The integration
acts on a 96-component vector (global) or on the 256 coarsest clusters (local and hybrid); the
fine mesh is visited by the condition trunk once per geometry and by the graph network once per
generated field. This is the main difference in cost from generators that iterate on a latent
graph tied to the mesh, such as LDGN and SAR \cite{lino2025distributions,lino2026sar}.

\section{Numerical examples}\label{sec:numerical}

In this section, we evaluate HI-MGN-V on a shell-buckling problem in which an unmeasured
geometric imperfection decides whether a nominal shell buckles. HI-MGN-V is compared with the
deterministic HI-MGN \cite{lee2026himgn} and with our ports of LDGN and SAR
\cite{lino2025distributions,lino2026sar}. The deterministic HI-MGN uses the same V-cycle as
HI-MGN-V and gives the mean-response reference. The hyperparameters of LDGN and SAR are set to the
values of the original authors, and only those hyperparameters that the authors themselves vary
between their examples are adapted. Within HI-MGN-V, the latent placement (global, local, hybrid)
and the regularization (MMD, KL) are studied as ablations.

% ======================================================================
\subsection{Axially compressed cylindrical shells with random imperfections}\label{sec:dataset}

We consider thin cylindrical shells under axial compression. Figure~\ref{fig:geom} illustrates the
configuration. The bottom rim is clamped in all six degrees of freedom, and the top rim is driven
axially by an end shortening $\Delta$ at a fixed speed of $0.25$\,mm/ms. The shell dimensions
vary across samples, characterized by a radius $R\in[100,200]$\,mm, a thickness $t\in[1,2]$\,mm, a
length $L\in[150,300]$\,mm and an end shortening $\Delta\in[1,3]$\,mm, which are selected via
Latin hypercube sampling with 150 geometries. The classical buckling load is
$P_{cr}=3.803\,Et^2$ and the classical shortening $d_{cr}=0.60523\,tL/R$; the load ratio is
$\lambda=\Delta/d_{cr}$. The shells are linear elastic steel without yield, discretized with
structured quadrilateral shell elements, and solved with the explicit solver OpenRadioss. The
dataset is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dataset}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/geometry_schematic.png}
\caption{Boundary conditions, shown at the centre of the design box ($R=150$, $t=1.5$ (drawn
$8\times$ thicker), $L=225$\,mm). The bottom rim is clamped in all six degrees of freedom. The
top rim is driven axially by $\Delta$ at $0.25$\,mm/ms. The reaction force is an output, not an
input. $P_{cr}=3.803\,Et^2$ is 1797\,kN at the centre.}
\label{fig:geom}
\end{figure}

Each geometry is solved 64 times, each time with a different Gaussian dimple in its initial shape
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dimples}). The dimple has three random variables: its amplitude
$a\sim\N(0,0.4^2)$\,mm, its angular position, uniform over the circumference, and its axial
position, uniform at least $2\sigma$ away from both rims, where the dimple width $\sigma$ equals
the classical axisymmetric half-wavelength $l_c$. The model sees only the nominal,
imperfection-free cylinder, and the imperfection parameters are stored for diagnosis only.
Depending on the dimple, the same nominal shell stays unbuckled or buckles.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]

\paragraph{Buckling indicator} A draw is counted as buckled when its peak displacement
$s=\max_i\|\mathbf{u}_i\|$ exceeds 5.17\,mm. The threshold lies between the largest unbuckled and
the smallest buckled peak and agrees with the solver's buckling flag on 97.5\% of the FE draws.
Figure~\ref{fig:diag}(b) shows that the 64 draws of one geometry near the critical load give a
bimodal peak displacement, and Fig.~\ref{fig:diag}(c) that the buckling probability goes from 0 to
1 over $\lambda\approx0.6$--$1.05$ across geometries. The scatter of the response is thus decided
by the imperfection, and its mean is not a possible response.

\paragraph{Where the buckle forms} The dimple seeds the buckle but does not fix where it lies. In
the buckled FE draws of the out-of-range geometries, the node of largest radial displacement lies
within one dimple width $\sigma$ of the dimple centre, in angle and in axial position, in only
5--27\% of the draws of a geometry, against 1--5\% for a position chosen at random. The radial
lobes above 80\% of the peak cover a median of 6--14 of 18 angular sectors, so a buckle usually
spreads around much of the circumference. A buckled field therefore does not reveal its
imperfection, and a generated field is compared with independent FE results of the same nominal
design, not with a re-analysis of an imperfection read off the field.

\subsection{Training and inference settings}\label{sec:setup}

For training, we consider a three-level hierarchy. Letting $N$ be the number of nodes in the
training mesh, HI-MGN-V and HI-MGN are configured with the hierarchy $[N, 4096, 256]$ and the block
configuration $[4, 6, 8, 6, 4]$, i.e., 28 message passing blocks. Two hierarchy variants are
precomputed per training sample. The latent variable uses $D=32$ per level for $\zg$ and $C=4$
channels per coarsest cluster for $\zl$. During training, random rotations about the cylinder
axis and random reflections in $x$ and $y$ are applied to the training geometries, as the
boundary conditions and the imperfection distribution are preserved under these
transformations; no noise is added to the inputs. The remaining hyperparameters are kept identical
across the placements: 360 epochs with the prior-only phase starting at epoch 240, a batch size of
16, a latent dimension of 128, the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ and a weight
decay of $10^{-4}$, a linear warmup over 5 epochs followed by cosine annealing, gradient
clipping at a norm of 3.0, an EMA of the weights with a factor of 0.99, and bfloat16
mixed-precision training. The prior-only phase restarts Adam with its own warmup and cosine
schedule. Validation runs every 10 epochs on the 15 validation geometries, and the EMA weights are
used for validation and inference. The full configuration is listed in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.
All HI-MGN-V models are trained on a single NVIDIA B300 GPU.
% TODO(authors): add the GPU/CPU of the HI-MGN, LDGN and SAR runs, and training times.

For inference, we consider unseen geometries inside and outside the training range. For every FE
draw of an evaluation geometry, the model generates 4 fields from the prior with the hierarchy
built from a fixed seed. Since the draws of a geometry share the nominal input, the generated
fields of a geometry are pooled: 256 fields for a test geometry with 64 FE draws, and 1024 fields
for an out-of-range geometry with 256 FE draws.

\subsection{Compared models}\label{sec:baselines}

\paragraph{HI-MGN} The deterministic HI-MGN uses the same inputs, hierarchy, V-cycle and training
settings as HI-MGN-V, without the latent variable, and is trained by the MSE alone. It returns one
field per geometry and serves as the mean-response reference.

\paragraph{LDGN and SAR} The two generative baselines are ports of the authors' released code
\cite{lino2025distributions,lino2026sar} into the same data pipeline. In both, a geometry is one
system, and its FE draws take the place of the flow states of the original works. Both receive
the same inputs and split as HI-MGN-V and generate the final displacement field; the imperfection
parameters are never read. The models, their two training stages (a graph VAE, then a generative
model in its latent space), their mesh coarsening and their samplers are unchanged. SAR uses the
authors' wing configuration: a single-scale VAE of two layers and width 128 with a KL weight of
$10^{-6}$, and three scale-autoregressive iterations of 8 layers each with width 128, 64 slices
and 8 heads, sampled with 20, 11 and 2 steps. LDGN uses a three-level VAE of depths 2, 2, 1 and
width 126 with a one-channel latent field and a KL weight of $10^{-6}$, and a denoiser of depths 1,
2, 2, 2 and width 128 with 1000 diffusion steps of a linear schedule, learned variances and a
variational-bound weight of $10^{-3}$, sampled with 50 steps. Smaller batches with gradient
accumulation reproduce the authors' effective batch sizes, and both models train until the
learning rate of the authors' plateau schedule falls below its stopping threshold; the epoch
limits only cap the wall-clock time. The targets are scaled to $[-1,1]$ by their training minimum
and maximum, as in the original code; a variant with standardized targets is also run. Before
the shell problem, both ports were trained on the authors' wing dataset and reproduce the
published results: the median ratio of the field Wasserstein distance of SAR to that of the
released model is 1.02, and LDGN reaches an $R^2$ of the ensemble mean of 0.9962 (released 0.9965)
and a field Wasserstein distance of 2.11 (released 1.99).

\paragraph{HI-MGN-V ablations} The six HI-MGN-V variants are \{global, local, hybrid\} $\times$
\{MMD, KL\}. They share every other setting of Section~\ref{sec:setup}; only the latent placement
and the regularization (with its weight) differ. The hybrid placement with MMD is the reference
configuration.

Every metric is computed geometry by geometry, because the scatter within one geometry is
bimodal and its shape changes with $\lambda$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:diag}). The primary quantity is the
peak displacement $s=\max_i\|\mathbf{u}_i\|$ of each field, which contains both the axial
shortening and the radial buckling displacement.

\paragraph{Wasserstein distance of the peak displacement} For one geometry, let $F$ and $\hat F$ be
the empirical distributions of $s$ over the FE draws and over the generated fields, with means
$m,\hat m$ and standard deviations $\sigma,\hat\sigma$. The one-dimensional 2-Wasserstein distance
is computed exactly from the quantile functions and normalized by the FE standard deviation of
that geometry. It decomposes exactly into a bias, a spread and a shape term,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{W_2^2(\hat F,F)}{\sigma^2}
  = \underbrace{\Bigl(\frac{\hat m-m}{\sigma}\Bigr)^2}_{\text{bias}}
  + \underbrace{\Bigl(\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sigma}-1\Bigr)^2}_{\text{spread}}
  + \underbrace{2\,\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sigma}\,(1-\rho)}_{\text{shape}},
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the correlation between the two quantile functions. The score of a set is the root
mean square of $W_2/\sigma$ over its geometries, reported $\times100$. Even a model that samples
from the FE distribution does not score zero with a finite number of draws. On the out-of-range
set, the 256 FE draws of every geometry are therefore split at random into two halves of 128; the
first half is scored against the second as if it were generated, which gives the value that FE
results alone reach, and the model is scored with 128 of its generated fields against the same
second half. Over 200 random splits, the FE-alone value has a median of 27 (5--95\%: 20--34),
and a model below this range cannot be distinguished from the FE scatter.

\paragraph{Further scalar scores} For $s$, we also report the CRPS of the generated ensemble
against each FE draw, normalized by $\sigma$; the spread ratio $\hat\sigma/\sigma$ (1 is correct,
below 1 is too little scatter); and the buckling-probability error $|P_{\text{model}}-P_{\text{FE}}|$
with the threshold of 5.17\,mm. Results are shown per geometry as histograms of the generated
peak displacements beside the FE histograms, not only as averaged scores.

\paragraph{Posterior versus prior} Before any comparison of models, the trained graph network
generates two sets of fields for each geometry: one from latent vectors inferred from the FE
results by the encoder (posterior), one from latent vectors drawn from the prior. If the
posterior set matches the FE results and the prior set does not, the prior is at fault; if both
fail, the graph network or the encoder is. This check tells which part of the model a
difference belongs to.

\paragraph{Field-level scores} For the out-of-range geometries, the generated fields are also
compared with the FE fields as whole fields, on the same mesh. For a generated field $\hat\by$ and
an FE result $\by$, $R^2(\hat\by\mid\by)=1-\sum_i\|\hat\by_i-\by_i\|^2/\sum_i\|\by_i-\bar\by\|^2$
and $d(\hat\by,\by)=(\frac1N\sum_i\|\hat\by_i-\by_i\|^2)^{1/2}$. Since the cylinder, its boundary
conditions and the imperfection distribution are axisymmetric, a turn of an FE result about the
axis by a multiple of the angular mesh pitch is an FE result of the same design, and
$R^2_{\circlearrowleft}$ takes the best of these turns. Sample realism (a) is measured in both
directions,
\begin{equation}
  A_{\text{real}}=\frac1n\sum_{j}\max_{k}R^2_{\circlearrowleft}(\hat\by_j\mid\by_k),\qquad
  A_{\text{cov}}=\frac1K\sum_{k}\max_{j}R^2_{\circlearrowleft}(\hat\by_j\mid\by_k),
\end{equation}
which ask whether every generated field is close to some FE result and whether every FE result is
close to some generated field, as precision and recall \cite{kynkaanniemi2019improved}; the first
direction is the nearest-state $R^2$ of LDGN and SAR \cite{lino2025distributions,lino2026sar}.
Distribution match (b) is measured by the field Wasserstein distance
\begin{equation}
  W_2^{\text{field}}=\Big(\min_{\pi}\frac1n\sum_{j}d(\hat\by_j,\by_{\pi(j)})^2\Big)^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where $\pi$ runs over the one-to-one assignments of $n$ generated fields to $n$ FE results and is
found exactly by the Hungarian algorithm; no turn is taken out, since a model that puts every
buckle at the same angle must fail (b). Both are reported beside the values that the FE results
alone reach with the same number of samples, from the same split into halves of 128
\cite{luthen2023spectral,kansal2023evaluating}. Out-of-range inference (c) is the repetition of
(a) and (b) on the out-of-range set.
